I. 
The Securities and Exchange Commission (“Commission”) deems it appropriate that cease-
and-desist proceedings be, and hereby are, instituted pursuant to Section 8A of the Securities Act 
of 1933 (“Securities Act”) and Section 21C of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 (“Exchange 
Act”), against Latch, Inc. (“Latch” or “Respondent”). 
 
II. 
In anticipation of the institution of these proceedings, Respondent has submitted an Offer 
of Settlement (the “Offer”) which the Commission has determined to accept.  Solely for the 
purpose of these proceedings and any other proceedings brought by or on behalf of the 
Commission, or to which the Commission is a party, and without admitting the findings herein, 
except as to the Commission’s jurisdiction over it and the subject matter of these proceedings, 
which are admitted, Respondent consents to the entry of this Order Instituting Cease-and-Desist 
Proceedings Pursuant to Section 8A of the Securities Act of 1933 and Section 21C of the Securities 
Exchange Act of 1934, Making Findings, and Imposing a Cease-and-Desist Order (“Order”), as set 
forth below.

III. 
On the basis of this Order and Respondent’s Offer, the Commission finds1 that:   
 
Summary 
1. These proceedings arise out of revenue recognition errors by Respondent, a 
Missouri-based manufacturer of smart access hardware and software products, that spanned from 
at least January 2019 through March 2022 (the “Relevant Period”).  Respondent became a public 
company through a June 2021 business combination (the “De-SPAC Transaction”) with a special 
purpose acquisition company (the “SPAC”) that resulted in Respondent obtaining approximately 
$450 million in cash proceeds from investors.  Respondent materially misrepresented its 2019 and 
2020 revenue in financial statements that were included in registration statements and proxy filings 
filed with the Commission and provided to investors in connection with the De-SPAC Transaction.  
After Respondent became publicly traded, it continued to misstate its revenue, including in its 2021 
Form 10-K and interim period reports and associated Forms 8-K.   
2. Respondent grew rapidly following its first commercial sale in 2016, but its internal 
accounting control environment did not keep pace.  Respondent had an aggressive sales culture 
that prioritized meeting internal targets by shipping hardware— often in advance of organic 
customer demand and through the use of side agreements— meaning agreements not contained in 
the purchase order or sales order.  Notwithstanding the use of side agreements and its aggressive 
sales culture that resulted in sales being pulled forward, Respondent failed to devise and maintain a 
sufficient system of internal accounting controls to detect the agreements or make sure that key 
details regarding these sales were available to permit preparation of its financial statements in 
accordance with U.S. generally accepted accounting principles (“GAAP”).  Respondent also failed 
to devise and maintain a sufficient system of internal accounting controls to:  (1) assess the 
collectability of its contracts with distributors, resulting in Respondent recognizing revenue for 
sales through distributors who did not have the ability to pay; and (2) ensure timely collection of 
sell-through pricing data to incorporate into its revenue recognition transaction price determination 
certain discounts it provided.  As a result, investors and market participants lacked accurate 
information to make informed investment decisions. 
3. Following an internal investigation into these and other issues, Respondent filed an 
annual report in December 2024 for the year ended December 31, 2022 (the “Restated 10-K”), 
which disclosed that Respondent had overstated its revenue for the Relevant Period, including by 
approximately 107% for the year ended December 31, 2019; 39% for the year ended December 31, 
2020; and 50% for the year ended December 31, 2021 (as well as in interim periods).  
4. Based on the foregoing and the conduct described below, Respondent violated 
Sections 17(a)(2) and 17(a)(3) of the Securities Act and Sections 13(a), 13(b)(2)(A), 13(b)(2)(B), 
and 14(a) of the Exchange Act and Rules 13a-1, 13a-11, 13a-13, and 14a-9 thereunder.

Respondent 
5. Respondent is a Delaware corporation based in Olivette, Missouri that develops and 
manufactures smart access hardware and software products for multi-family residential properties.  
From its founding in 2014 until June 2021, Respondent was privately held.  Respondent announced 
a merger with the SPAC in January 2021 and became publicly traded on June 7, 2021.  From June 
2021 to June 2024, Respondent’s common stock and warrants were registered pursuant to Section 
12(b) of the Exchange Act and, from June 2021 to April 2024, traded on the Nasdaq Global Select 
Market under the tickers “LTCH” and “LTCHW,” respectively.  Respondent’s common stock and 
warrants are registered pursuant to Section 12(g) of the Exchange Act and are traded on the OTC 
Link ATS. 
Facts 
Background 
6. Respondent began selling smart locks in or around 2016, targeting the multi-family 
residential industry as its primary market and building developers and operators as its customers.  
Respondent entered into non-binding letters of intent (“LOI”) with those developers that set forth, 
among other things, the quantity and price for Respondent’s hardware and software as well as a 
target delivery date for anticipated installation.  In most cases, however, Respondent delivered the 
locks to a sales agent (referred to as a channel partner) that had the expertise to install and activate 
them.  During the Relevant Period, most of Respondent’s revenue came from hardware sales made 
through channel partners. 
7. On January 24, 2021, Respondent signed a merger agreement with the SPAC.  
Respondent’s 2019 and 2020 financial statements, which contained its materially overstated 
revenues, were included in filings made with the Commission, including a Form S-4 filed on 
March 10, 2021, four amendments filed on March 30, 2021, May 3, 2021, May 10, 2021, and May 
12, 2021, and a May 13, 2021 prospectus/proxy statement.  The SPAC’s shareholders approved the 
merger and the transaction closed on June 4, 2021.  The transaction resulted in Respondent 
obtaining approximately $450 million in cash proceeds. 
8. Additionally, after it became a publicly traded company in June 2021, Respondent 
reported materially overstated revenue in its June 25, 2021 Form S-1 registration statement, as well 
as related July 7, 2021 and March 4, 2022 amendments, and in its current and periodic reports filed 
with the Commission from the time it became a publicly traded company through the first quarter 
of 2022. 
Respondent’s Revenue Recognition Policy and its Application 
9. Respondent disclosed in public filings that it prepared its financial statements in 
accordance with GAAP and, more specifically, that it recognized revenue consistent with 
Accounting Standards Codification Topic 606, Revenue from Contracts with Customers (“ASC 
606”).  Respondent also disclosed that it recognized hardware revenue when the hardware was 
shipped to its customers or channel partners, which was when Latch determined that control over 
the product transferred to the customer, in an amount that reflected the consideration it expected to 
receive in exchange for those products.   
4 
10. Respondent could not, and did not, account for certain of its hardware sales 
transactions in accordance with ASC 606 and its own accounting policies, in large part because it 
had not devised and maintained a sufficient system of internal accounting controls to reasonably 
assure that all relevant sales terms and other pertinent sale-related information were obtained and 
considered by the appropriate personnel to determine whether, when, and in what amount to 
recognize revenue. 
Respondent Failed to Consider or Account for Sales Terms in Side Agreements 
11. Respondent typically received binding purchase orders from channel partners that 
contained the terms of the transaction.  Respondent also had policies governing its relationships 
with channel partners that established certain standard terms and generally required channel 
partners to submit purchase orders for specific projects.   
12. Throughout the Relevant Period, Respondent’s sales personnel aggressively sought 
purchase orders from channel partners, which allowed Respondent to ship product, to meet short-
term sales targets and hit personal sales quotas for commissions.  Sales personnel often offered 
nonstandard arrangements to induce channel partners to submit purchase orders for products before 
the date Respondent’s end customer had indicated it needed the product for installation.  These 
nonstandard terms were documented, if at all, in side agreements and included, at times, 
unconditional rights to return products; conditional or extended payment terms; obtaining purchase 
orders from, and shipping hardware to, channel partners without specified customer projects; and 
providing free storage.  Because Latch recognized revenue upon shipment to the channel partner, 
these practices, at times, caused Latch to improperly recognize revenue before control transferred 
to the customer.  
13. Despite the systemic nature of these practices, Respondent failed to devise and 
maintain a sufficient system of internal accounting controls to determine when revenue could be 
properly recognized.  Respondent did not have sufficient internal accounting controls to identify 
side agreements and provide reasonable assurance that all relevant sales terms and other pertinent 
sale-related information were obtained and considered by the appropriate personnel to determine 
whether, when, and in what amount to recognize revenue.  For example, Respondent’s sales 
personnel were supposed to enter relevant sales terms into a sales system and could upload notes 
and documents relating to side agreements.  Those terms then migrated to an accounting system for 
review and approval by finance and accounting.  However, Respondent had no sufficient 
mechanism that provided reasonable assurance that sales personnel actually entered side agreement 
information into the sales system so that it was available to finance and accounting personnel for 
consideration in accounting determinations and, in practice, they rarely, if ever, did.  As a result, 
Respondent’s accounting team did not have all relevant information to account for certain sales 
transactions appropriately. 
14. Respondent’s sales practices, coupled with its internal accounting control failures, 
resulted in significant revenue recognition issues.  Many sales transactions were recorded in a 
manner inconsistent with ASC 606 and Respondent’s own accounting policies.  As a result, in 
some instances, Respondent recognized revenue that should not have been recognized in any 
period, while in many other instances Respondent recognized revenue in incorrect periods. 
5 
15. For example, on or around December 9, 2020, in the last month of the quarter, 
Respondent shipped $147,000 of hardware to a channel partner conditioned on it having the right 
to return the hardware without consequence if the building developer did not move forward with its 
project—a nonstandard term that was memorialized in a side agreement and not provided to 
Respondent’s finance and accounting team.  Because of the side agreement, contrary to ASC 606 
and its own accounting policies, Respondent improperly recognized $147,000 in revenue relating 
to this transaction in the fourth quarter of 2020.  In or around June 2021, the channel partner 
returned the hardware. 
16. In another instance, in March 2021, days before the end of the quarter, Respondent 
shipped approximately $474,000 of hardware to a channel partner conditioned on it having the 
right to return the hardware without consequence.  Sales personnel also agreed that the channel 
partner could pay for the hardware after it received payment from the building developer, the 
timing of which was uncertain.  These side agreements were not shared with Respondent’s finance 
and accounting team.  Contrary to ASC 606 and its own accounting policies, Respondent 
improperly recognized approximately $474,000 in revenue from the transaction in the first quarter 
of 2021.  In fact, Respondent concluded as part of its restatement process that the revenue 
associated with this transaction should have been recognized in later periods, including 
approximately $350,000 in the second quarter of 2023 or later— more than two years after 
Respondent initially recognized it.   
Respondent Failed to Assess Collectability 
17. Respondent’s ASC 606 accounting policies reflected, among other things, that 
revenue could be recognized if it was probable the company would collect substantially all of the 
consideration it was owed by the customer for that transaction.  However, Respondent failed to 
devise and maintain a sufficient system of internal accounting controls to assess collectability.  
Respondent, therefore, lacked a reasonable basis to determine that it could recognize revenue under 
ASC 606 and its own accounting policies.   
18. For example, during the Relevant Period, Respondent recognized as revenue nearly 
$5.7 million from a single channel partner while collecting only $0.3 million in cash.  The channel 
partner, as agent for customers, was responsible for payment to Respondent.  Respondent had 
significant past due accounts receivable for this channel partner during this period but continued to 
recognize revenue for hardware that was shipped to it without a reasonable basis to conclude that 
collectability would be assured.  Respondent concluded in its restatement process that, had it 
devised and maintained a sufficient system of internal controls to assess collectability, it likely 
would not have extended additional credit to the channel partner and, as a result, determined that it 
recognized revenue in contravention of both GAAP and its own accounting policies.  
Respondent Recognized Revenue Based on an Improper Transaction Price 
19. During the Relevant Period, Respondent offered price discounts— referred to 
internally as distributor cuts— to channel partners who acted as sales agents.  These discounts were 
often in excess of 20% of the hardware’s retail price.  Channel partners could pass through all or a 
portion of the distributor cut to Respondent’s end customers, the building developers or operators.   
6 
20. In its ASC 606 accounting policies, Respondent concluded that the channel partners 
were agents in sales transactions with building developers and operators.  Respondent determined 
it could recognize as revenue the full transaction price paid by the developer, with the distributor 
cut recorded as a cost of sales.  Respondent’s ability to determine its transaction price, including 
estimating any variable consideration, was premised on Respondent having access to the sell-
through price that the building developer or operator ultimately paid for Respondent’s hardware.  
Respondent’s accounting policies acknowledged that in instances where it did not, or could not, 
obtain sell-through pricing data, it should exclude the distributor cuts from the transaction price 
and recognize revenue at the price paid to Respondent by the channel partner.  
21. Respondent failed to devise and maintain a system of internal accounting controls 
sufficient to provide reasonable assurance that it received the necessary sell-through pricing data.  
From 2019 through August 2021, Respondent’s policies required channel partners to report sell-
through pricing data in response to a request from Respondent.  However, Respondent rarely, if 
ever, requested sell-through pricing data from its channel partners.  Starting in August of 2021, 
Respondent modified its policies to require channel partners to include sell-through pricing data in 
their purchase orders.  Respondent, however, did nothing to ensure or otherwise enforce its policy 
that sell-through pricing data be included in channel partners’ purchase orders.  Instead, 
Respondent improperly relied on the pricing terms established in the non-binding LOIs that 
Respondent entered into with building developers.  This was improper because channel partners 
had the ability to modify the price charged to the developers. 
22. Respondent lacked sufficient sell-through pricing data to support its estimate of the 
variable consideration in the transaction price in contravention of ASC 606 and its own internal 
accounting policies.  In its Restated 10-K, Respondent identified that transaction price errors 
resulted in hardware sales revenue overstatements in the amounts of $3.7 million, $4.1 million, and 
$4.5 million for the years ended December 31, 2019, 2020, and 2021, respectively.  
Respondent’s Internal Investigation and Restatement 
23. In mid-2022, in response to allegations that Respondent had engaged in improper 
sales practices, the Audit Committee of Respondent’s Board of Directors (“Audit Committee”) 
initiated an internal investigation into Respondent’s historical revenue recognition practices.  On 
August 10, 2022, Respondent disclosed its investigation in a Form 12b-25 Notification of Late 
Filing.  Respondent filed periodic updates about the investigation and, on January 23, 2023, filed a 
Form 8-K stating that its investigation was substantially complete.  Respondent disclosed that its 
financial statements for the years 2019 and 2020, its 2021 annual report, and the quarterly reports 
for each of the second, third, and fourth quarters of 2021 and the first quarter of 2022, were 
materially misstated, should no longer be relied upon, and would be restated.   
24. In its January 23, 2023 Form 8-K, Respondent identified three primary causes of its 
overstated revenue:  (1) a failure to disclose relevant terms negotiated in side agreements with 
channel partners and a failure to identify, consider, or properly account for such terms, (2) a failure 
to consider fully the impact of certain terms of sales agreements with channel partners in 
determining the amount and timing of revenue to be recognized, and (3) a failure to adequately 
assess collectability.

Violations 
 
26. As a result of the conduct described above, Respondent violated Section 17(a)(2) of 
the Securities Act, which proscribes, in the offer or sale of a security, obtaining “money or property 
by means of any untrue statement of a material fact or any omission to state a material fact 
necessary in order to make the statements made, in light of the circumstances under which they 
were made, not misleading.”  In addition, as a result of the conduct described above, Respondent 
violated Section 17(a)(3) of the Securities Act, which proscribes, in the offer or sale of a security, 
engaging “in any transaction, practice, or course of business which operates or would operate as a 
fraud or deceit upon the purchaser.” 
27. As a result of the conduct described above, Respondent also violated Section 13(a) 
of the Exchange Act and Rules 13a-1, 13a-11, and 13a-13 thereunder, which require every issuer 
of a security registered pursuant to Section 12 of the Exchange Act to file annual, current, and 
quarterly reports with the Commission in accordance with such rules and regulations as the 
Commission may prescribe. 
28. As a result of the conduct described above, Respondent also violated Section 
13(b)(2)(A) of the Exchange Act, which requires reporting companies to make and keep books, 
records, and accounts which, in reasonable detail, accurately and fairly reflect their transactions 
and dispositions of their assets. 
29. As a result of the conduct described above, Respondent also violated Section 
13(b)(2)(B) of the Exchange Act, which requires reporting companies to devise and maintain a 
system of internal accounting controls sufficient to provide reasonable assurances that transactions 
are recorded as necessary to permit preparation of financial statements in conformity with GAAP 
or any other criteria applicable to such statements. 
8 
30. Lastly, as a result of the conduct described above, Respondent violated Section 
14(a) of the Exchange Act and Rule 14a-9 thereunder, which prohibit the use of proxy statements 
containing materially false or misleading statements or omissions of material fact necessary to 
make statements not misleading.   
Respondent’s Cooperation and Remedial Efforts 
31. In determining to accept the Offer, the Commission considered Respondent’s 
cooperation with the staff’s investigation and its remedial efforts after discovering the violations at 
issue. 
32. After learning of potential misconduct, Respondent retained outside counsel to 
conduct an internal investigation under the direction and oversight of its Audit Committee.  
Respondent reported on the findings of the investigation to Commission staff and provided 
selected documents to illustrate certain findings.  Upon request, Respondent also provided staff 
with additional documents and with explanations and summaries of specific factual issues.  
Respondent, including its leadership, also met with the staff to discuss its restatement and related 
issues.   
33. Respondent also undertook remedial measures relating to the conduct described in 
the Offer of Settlement, including (i)  making changes to management, the finance, sales, and 
accounting teams; (ii) developing training for Respondent personnel; and (iii) implementing new 
controls relating to Respondent’s accounting, monitoring, risk assessment, and financial reporting 
functions, including revising its Sarbanes-Oxley compliance program. 
IV. 
In view of the foregoing, the Commission deems it appropriate and in the public interest to 
impose the sanctions agreed to in Respondent Latch’s Offer. 
Accordingly, it is hereby ORDERED that: 
A. Pursuant to Section 8A of the Securities Act and Section 21C of the Exchange Act, 
Respondent Latch cease and desist from committing or causing any violations and any future 
violations of Sections 17(a)(2) and 17(a)(3) of the Securities Act and Sections 13(a), 13(b)(2)(A), 
13(b)(2)(B), and 14(a) of the Exchange Act and Rules 13a-1, 13a-11, 13a-13, and 14a-9 thereunder. 
B. Respondent shall pay a civil penalty of $1,000,000, to the Securities and Exchange 
Commission for transfer to the general fund of the United States Treasury, subject to Exchange Act 
Section 21F(g)(3).  If timely payment is not made, additional interest shall accrue pursuant to 31 
U.S.C. § 3717.  Payment shall be made in four quarterly installments of $250,000, the first of 
which is due within twenty (20) days of the entry of this Order, and the three remaining 
installments are due, one per quarter, on or before the last day of the subsequent three quarters.  
Payments shall be applied first to post order interest, which accrues pursuant to 31 U.S.C. § 3717.

Commission for the amount due.  If Respondent fails to make any payment by the date agreed 
and/or in the amount agreed according to the schedule set forth above, all outstanding payments
